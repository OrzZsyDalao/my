\section{Robustness, Scope, and Implications}
\label{sec:scope}

This section tests the main result across candidate-allocation and landing-
region resolutions, then interprets the geographic contrast and its
measurement implications.

\subsection{Candidate-Allocation Robustness}
\label{subsec:allocation-robustness}

Candidate allocation changes the magnitude of Layer Agreement but not the
geographic ordering in the existing results. As Table
~\ref{tab:geographic-agreement} reports, the island--coastal coefficient
difference is 0.461 under equal shares, 0.631 under projection-score weighting,
and 0.715 under Top-1 allocation. The corresponding island coefficients are
0.380, 0.632, and 0.820; the coastal-mainland coefficients are -0.081, 0.001,
and 0.105.

The allocation-specific coastal-mainland cohorts differ by three units. Equal
sharing retains 205 coastal-mainland units; projection-score and Top-1 retain
202. For equal sharing, country-cluster 95\% intervals are [-0.203, 0.760] for
island and archipelagic units and [-0.284, 0.238] for coastal-mainland units.
The interval for their 0.461 difference is [-0.213, 0.902], and the two-sided
country-label permutation value is $p=0.161$. The wide interval includes zero
and quantifies uncertainty across the 10 island and 30 coastal country
clusters in the equal-share unit table.

Equal allocation spreads each segment's mass across every feasible corridor,
projection-score weighting places more mass on better-supported candidates,
and Top-1 retains only the highest-scoring candidate. All three rules produce
the same geographic ordering, so the contrast is robust to these alternative
treatments of candidate ambiguity. Across the available allocation-specific cohorts, the increasing island
coefficients are consistent with higher-scoring candidates carrying more of
the cross-layer signal.

\subsection{Landing-Region Resolution}
\label{subsec:resolution-sensitivity}

The A-Root landing-region analysis varies maximum diameter over 10, 20, 30,
40, and 50~km while holding the endpoint catchment at 50~km and the RTT
tolerance at 5~ms. Table~\ref{tab:diameter-sensitivity} records changes in the
candidate-bearing population, auditable-unit count, single-corridor share, and
median Top-2 share. Exact landing-pair and cable candidate sets have mean
Jaccard 1.0 on segments shared with the 30-km baseline. The sweep therefore
confirms unchanged exact candidates on shared segments while recording how
region grouping, candidate-bearing population, and auditable-unit count change
together within the A-Root subset.

The sweep shows a clear resolution transition. The 10--30-km settings retain
a finer coastal partition, whereas single-corridor share rises to 90.1\% and
94.8\% at 40 and 50~km and median Top-2 share reaches 81.6\% and 84.9\%.
We use 30~km because it remains on the fine side of this observed transition

an empirical basis for the reporting resolution within the tested A-Root
subset and an operational basis for the 30-km setting used throughout the
audit.

\subsection{Dependence on Geolocation and Candidate Construction}
\label{subsec:geolocation-scope}

Every projection begins with IPinfo hop geolocation and the fixed 50-km
endpoint catchment. Geographic, lifecycle, and propagation constraints then
construct the feasible candidate set. Cable lifecycle filtering aligns the
inventory with July~1, 2026; a recorded inventory refresh changed 222 of 694
cable records, including 26 planned-flag changes and 47 revised service dates.
The output retains exact landing-pair, cable, corridor, and mapping-resolution
identities, making each projected distribution traceable to its geolocation
and inventory inputs.

\subsection{Snapshot and Population Scope}
\label{subsec:measurement-scope}

All 18 measurements cover the same 00:00--01:00 UTC interval on July~1, 2026.
Routing state, service deployment, cable inventory, hop geolocation, and AS
annotations are aligned to this common window. The resulting cross-section
therefore compares every measurement and geographic group under the same
observed network state.

Reported quantities are observation-weighted over participating RIPE Atlas
probes and therefore represent the probes and source ASNs present in each
country--measurement population. The eligibility thresholds of 10 probes and
3 probe ASNs remove sparse units from the paired analysis.

The service measurements retain their selected instances, while the dynamic
multi-target references deliberately sample many destinations during the same
window. The reference distributions therefore provide a broader-target
context for the service-facing populations. Anycast observations jointly
reflect deployed footprint, BGP selection, interconnection, and source-
network context.

\subsection{Implications for Diversity Measurement}
\label{subsec:resilience-implications}

Layer Agreement separates two questions that a network-layer count can
otherwise combine. Network Top-2 share records concentration among observed
network transitions. Corridor Top-2 share records concentration among feasible
physical directions for the same candidate-bearing segments. In this snapshot,
the association between the quantities is stronger for island and
archipelagic service-facing units than for coastal-mainland units under all
three allocation rules.

The existing absolute-breadth results rule out a simple explanation based on
island units occupying a universally narrower corridor space. In the DNS
country-level summary, island and archipelagic countries have a lower median
corridor Top-2 share than other coastal countries (53.3\% versus 75.4\%) and a
higher median effective corridor count (5.58 versus 3.39). Their higher Layer
Agreement therefore concerns how network and corridor concentration rankings
move together, not uniformly higher corridor concentration.

One mechanism consistent with the measurements is that maritime access and
network organization impose partly shared constraints on the two rankings for
island and archipelagic sources, whereas terrestrial routing, domestic
backhaul, and interconnection allow network concentration to vary more
independently in coastal-mainland settings. The absolute-breadth results rule
out the simpler account that island units merely occupy a uniformly narrower
corridor space: island countries have lower median corridor Top-2 share and
more effective corridors than other coastal countries. Island and
archipelagic countries also enter the candidate space more often, but entry
frequency alone does not explain the stronger Layer Agreement. Across the 38
countries with both summaries, exposure has only -0.29 association with
corridor Top-2 share and 0.30 with effective corridor count. This pattern is therefore consistent with co-variation after entry rather than
a simple entry-frequency explanation.

The heterogeneous cross-layer classes make the result operationally
actionable at two levels. Geographic Layer Agreement identifies where network
diversity is informative about physical-corridor diversity: the signal is
strong for island and archipelagic units and nearly absent for coastal-
mainland units. Unit-level classes then identify 81 service-facing populations
with broad network support but concentrated corridor support, alongside 158
that remain broad at both layers. These quantities locate populations where a
network-layer diversity audit should be followed by physical-layer inspection.

Corridor concentration can be combined with cable multiplicity within a
corridor, landing-station redundancy, shared terrestrial backhaul, correlated
hazards, available rerouting capacity, and repair processes in a broader
resilience assessment. CLASP supplies the cross-layer concentration component
and preserves the exact candidate identities needed for that follow-on
analysis.

\subsection{Ethics and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:ethics-reproducibility}

The study uses archived public RIPE Atlas measurements and infrastructure
metadata and reports aggregate country--measurement statistics. It does not
target individual users or report individual probe addresses. Candidate
assignments are feasible hypotheses and should not be interpreted as
disclosure of an operator's actual cable use.

The released measurement IDs, configuration values, mapping-resolution
states, pipeline counts, and scripts reconstruct the primary tables and
figures from versioned inputs. The paper's 80\% four-class rule is the primary
descriptive classification; the repository's tier-based auxiliary
classification uses a different rule and must remain separately named in the
artifact.
