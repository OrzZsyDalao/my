\section{Robustness, Scope, and Implications}
\label{sec:scope}

The cross-layer distributions are feasible-corridor estimates rather than
observations of cable use. This section reports the robustness checks already
present in the experiment set and states which uncertainties remain
unmeasured. It then separates the measured quantities from their operational
interpretation.

\subsection{Candidate-Allocation Robustness}
\label{subsec:allocation-robustness}

Candidate allocation changes the magnitude of Layer Agreement but not the
geographic ordering in the existing results. As Table
~\ref{tab:geographic-agreement} reports, the island--coastal coefficient
difference is 0.461 under equal shares, 0.631 under projection-score weighting,
and 0.715 under Top-1 allocation. The corresponding island coefficients are
0.380, 0.632, and 0.820; the coastal-mainland coefficients are -0.081, 0.001,
and 0.105.

This comparison addresses one source of ambiguity directly. Equal allocation
spreads each segment's mass across every feasible corridor, projection-score
weighting places more mass on better-supported candidates, and Top-1 retains
only the highest-scoring candidate. The increasing coefficients show that the
estimated agreement depends on candidate allocation. The repeated geographic
ordering shows that the observed contrast is not produced by one of these
three rules. The experiment does not provide cable-use ground truth and
therefore does not establish which coefficient is closest to the physical
route actually used.

\subsection{Landing-Region Resolution}
\label{subsec:resolution-sensitivity}

The available landing-region-diameter analysis covers the A-Root subset, not
all 18 measurements. It varies maximum diameter over 10, 20, 30, 40, and
50~km while holding the endpoint catchment at 50~km and the RTT tolerance at
5~ms. Table~\ref{tab:diameter-sensitivity} reports the recorded values. Exact
landing-pair and cable candidate sets have mean Jaccard 1.0 on segments shared
with the 30-km baseline, but the candidate-bearing population and number of
auditable units change with diameter. The sensitivity therefore cannot be
described as isolating grouping resolution over a fixed global cohort.

recomputes the main result across endpoint catchment, propagation speed, or
RTT tolerance.

\subsection{Dependence on Geolocation and Candidate Construction}
\label{subsec:geolocation-scope}

Every corridor projection begins with hop geolocation. A hop placed farther
inland can remove a nearby landing candidate, while a location shifted toward
a coast can add one. The 50-km endpoint catchment is the fixed setting in the
reported analysis. The current experiment set contains no completed location-
perturbation analysis or alternative-geolocation comparison, so the effect of
residual IPinfo error is not bounded.

The projection also lacks physical-route ground truth. Geographic, lifecycle,
and propagation constraints identify feasible candidates rather than the
cable used by a packet. Exact landing-pair, cable, and corridor identities
remain linked in the output, permitting later inspection, but the available
materials contain no completed manual corridor audit. We therefore report
candidate support and avoid statements that require verified cable traversal.

Cable lifecycle filtering uses the inventory fields available for the
measurement date. These fields do not report transient failures or outages,
and the inventory can be revised. A recorded refresh changed 222 of 694 cable
records, including 26 planned-flag changes and 47 revised service dates. The
pre-refresh inventory was not used for an existing sensitivity result. Units
whose feasible sets lie near a metadata revision may consequently change with
the inventory.

\subsection{Snapshot and Population Scope}
\label{subsec:measurement-scope}

All 18 measurements cover 00:00--01:00 UTC on July~1, 2026. Routing, service
deployment, and cable availability can change outside this window, so the
reported distributions describe one aligned snapshot and do not establish
temporal stability. A failure-based validation would require comparable
traceroutes on both sides of a cable event, which this snapshot does not
provide.

Reported quantities are observation-weighted over participating RIPE Atlas
probes. Probe density varies across countries and networks, so a country's
distribution represents the probes and source ASNs present in the corpus, not
its user population or traffic volume. The eligibility thresholds of 10
probes and 3 probe ASNs limit sparse units but do not make the sample
representative.

The service measurements and dynamic multi-target references also contain
different target populations. The references deliberately sample many
destinations during the window, whereas each service measurement retains its
selected instances. Their differences characterize the observed populations;
they are not a matched estimate of a service-selection or deployment effect.
Anycast observations jointly reflect deployed footprint, BGP selection,
interconnection, and source-network context.

\subsection{Implications for Diversity Measurement}
\label{subsec:resilience-implications}

Layer Agreement separates two questions that a network-layer count can
otherwise combine. Network Top-2 share records concentration among observed
network transitions. Corridor Top-2 share records concentration among feasible
physical directions for the same candidate-bearing segments. In this snapshot,
the association between the quantities is stronger for island and
archipelagic service-facing units than for coastal-mainland units under all
three allocation rules.

One possible explanation is that island paths reach submarine infrastructure
through a smaller set of coastal gateways, whereas coastal-mainland paths can
include more terrestrial routing. The current measurements do not test that
mechanism. The geographic result is an association, not evidence that
topography, gateway structure, or terrestrial routing causes the difference.

The heterogeneous cross-layer classes also constrain operational conclusions.
A broad network distribution projects to a concentrated corridor distribution
in 81 service-facing units, but 158 remain broad at both layers and some units
expand. A universal conversion from network breadth to physical breadth is
therefore unsupported. Exposure and corridor concentration can identify where
paths enter the candidate space and how candidate-bearing observations are
distributed, but this study does not estimate the effect of deploying a closer
service instance, changing interconnection, or adding landing-region access.

Corridor concentration is not an operational resilience metric by itself. It
does not measure cable multiplicity within a corridor, landing-station
redundancy, shared terrestrial backhaul, correlated hazards, available
rerouting capacity, or repair processes. These dimensions are required before
a feasible-corridor distribution can support claims about failure tolerance.

\subsection{Ethics and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:ethics-reproducibility}

The study uses archived public RIPE Atlas measurements and infrastructure
metadata and reports aggregate country--measurement statistics. It does not
target individual users or report individual probe addresses. Candidate
assignments are feasible hypotheses and should not be interpreted as
disclosure of an operator's actual cable use.

The released measurement IDs, configuration values, mapping-resolution
states, pipeline counts, and scripts reconstruct the primary tables and
figures from versioned inputs. The paper's 80\% four-class rule is the primary
descriptive classification; the repository's tier-based auxiliary
classification uses a different rule and must remain separately named in the
artifact.
