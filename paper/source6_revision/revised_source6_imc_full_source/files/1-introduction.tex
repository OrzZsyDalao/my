\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Submarine cables form the physical backbone of the global Internet and carry
nearly all intercontinental traffic~\cite{telegeography_submarine_map}. Cable
failures can isolate regions, degrade remote-service access, and redirect
traffic toward longer or congested alternatives. Routes that share a cable or
coastal corridor can be affected by the same physical event even when their
network-layer paths appear different. Evaluating path diversity therefore
requires measuring whether logically distinct routes also occupy distinct
physical directions.

Path diversity is usually measured at the network layer. Traceroute records
routers and Autonomous System (AS) transitions, so distinct hops or AS paths
are counted as distinct alternatives. It does not identify the submarine
infrastructure that could carry those paths. Two paths that appear independent
in traceroute may leave a region through the same coast and rely on the same
small set of feasible corridors. Whether network-layer diversity corresponds
to physical-corridor diversity is therefore an empirical question.

Prior work supplies the cross-layer mappings needed to study this question.
Internet Atlas, InterTubes, and iGDB connect observed Internet links to long-
haul infrastructure; Nautilus associates IP links with candidate submarine
cables; Calypso maps routes using cable layouts, network relationships, and
inland connectivity; and Xaminer applies cross-layer maps to infrastructure
risk analysis~\cite{durairajan2013atlas,durairajan2015intertubes,
anderson2022igdb,ramanathan2023nautilus,wang2025calypso,
ramanathan2024xaminer}. These systems identify physical infrastructure that
could carry an individual link or path. We use such assignments to measure a
population-level property: whether concentration among observed network
transitions tracks concentration among feasible physical corridors.

We present \emph{CLASP}, a Cross-Layer Audit of Service Paths. CLASP normalizes
each traceroute into visible, geolocated hop-pair segments. Within every
country--measurement population, ordered endpoint-AS transitions form the
network distribution and feasible pairs of landing regions form the physical-
corridor distribution. Both distributions contain the same candidate-bearing
segments. Each segment contributes one unit of observation mass, allocated
among candidate corridors by the existing projection score. We call the
Spearman rank association between network and corridor concentration across a
reported group of units \emph{Layer Agreement}. The coefficient records an
observed monotonic association; it is not a causal or predictive model.

Three questions organize the measurement. \textbf{RQ1} asks how frequently
country--service paths contain a feasible inter-region submarine corridor.
\textbf{RQ2} asks whether network-layer concentration tracks feasible-corridor
concentration, and whether the association differs across geographic groups
and candidate-allocation rules. \textbf{RQ3} asks how much variation is hidden
by aggregate summaries: which populations contract, remain broad at both
layers, remain concentrated, or expand after projection.

We apply CLASP to 490,911 valid IPv4 traceroutes from 18 public RIPE Atlas
measurements. The corpus covers all 13 DNS Roots, Wikipedia, Reddit, Netflix
Assets, and two dynamic multi-target measurements used as topology references.
The aligned one-hour snapshot yields 3,291,063 atomic segments, of which
2,432,559 are mappable and 124,350 support at least one feasible inter-region
corridor. After audit-quality filtering, the analysis contains 370 country--
measurement units: 222 DNS Root, 53 application, and 95 topology-reference
units. Each unit retains its source-country and measurement identity.

The main result is a geographic difference in the observed Layer Agreement.
Under projection-score weighting, the coefficient is 0.632 among 67 island and
archipelagic service-facing units: units that are more concentrated at the
network layer also tend to be more concentrated across feasible corridors.
Among 202 coastal-mainland units, the coefficient is 0.001, so the network-
layer ordering contains essentially no information about the corridor
ordering. No uncertainty interval is available in the existing experiment
set, and we make no significance claim for either coefficient.

The geographic contrast remains in the same direction under all three
candidate-allocation rules already computed. Equal-share allocation gives
Layer Agreement of 0.380 for island and archipelagic units and -0.081 for
coastal-mainland units; projection-score weighting gives 0.632 and 0.001; and
Top-1 allocation gives 0.820 and 0.105. The corresponding between-group
differences are 0.461, 0.631, and 0.715. The measured association increases as
more observation mass is placed on higher-scoring candidates, but physical-
route ground truth is unavailable, so these results do not identify one rule
as the true assignment.

The service-facing population is also heterogeneous. Of 275 units, 81
(29.5\%) have a broad network distribution and a concentrated corridor
distribution, while 158 (57.5\%) remain broad at both layers. Eighteen are
concentrated at both layers and 18 expand from concentrated network support to
broad corridor support. Singapore--Netflix illustrates continuous narrowing:
its effective network-transition count of 19.86 becomes an effective corridor
count of 3.41 without crossing the 80\% concentration threshold. These outcomes
show why a single contraction rate cannot represent all populations.

This paper makes three contributions. First, CLASP constructs paired network-
transition and feasible-corridor distributions from identical traceroute
segments, separating trace-level exposure from conditional concentration.
Second, we measure a geographic difference in Layer Agreement that remains in
the same direction under three existing candidate-allocation rules. Third, we
provide a reproducible audit structure that records measurement IDs,
configuration values, mapping states, and pipeline counts, allowing the
reported distributions and their limitations to be inspected.
